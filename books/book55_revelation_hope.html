The island of Patmos was a prison without walls — a desolate rock in the Aegean Sea where Rome sent its most dangerous political prisoners. The apostle John, now elderly and weathered by decades of faithful ministry, had been exiled here for one crime: refusing to stop proclaiming that Jesus Christ is Lord. Everyone he had walked with was gone. Peter — crucified. Paul — beheaded. James — killed by Herod's sword. John was the last apostle standing, and the empire wanted him silenced on this forgotten island. But Rome had made a critical miscalculation. You can exile a prophet, but you cannot exile the God who speaks through him.

On the Lord's Day, John heard behind him a voice like a trumpet — thunderous, commanding, impossible to ignore. When he turned, he saw something that shattered every comfortable image he had ever held of his old friend Jesus. This was not the gentle rabbi who had leaned on his shoulder at the Last Supper. This was the glorified Christ — hair white as snow, eyes like blazing fire, feet like bronze refined in a furnace, voice like the roar of many waters. In His right hand He held seven stars, and from His mouth came a sharp double-edged sword. John, who had once rested his head on Jesus' chest, fell at His feet as though dead. The risen Christ is not merely our friend — He is the terrifying, beautiful, sovereign Lord of the universe.

"Do not be afraid," Jesus said, placing His right hand on John. "I am the First and the Last. I am the Living One; I was dead, and now look, I am alive forever and ever! And I hold the keys of death and Hades." Then Jesus dictated seven letters to seven churches — each one showing that He knew their struggles intimately. To Ephesus: "You have forsaken your first love." To Smyrna: "Be faithful even unto death." To Pergamum: "You dwell where Satan's throne is, yet you hold fast to My name." Jesus walks among His churches. He sees everything — the hidden faithfulness, the secret compromise, the slow drift, the quiet perseverance. Nothing escapes His burning eyes.

Then a door opened in heaven, and John was caught up into the throne room of God. What he saw there would have shattered every notion of worship he had ever known. A throne stood in heaven with One seated upon it, radiating jasper and carnelian light, encircled by an emerald rainbow. Twenty-four elders sat on surrounding thrones, casting their golden crowns before the throne. Four living creatures — covered in eyes, never resting — cried out day and night: "Holy, holy, holy is the Lord God Almighty, who was, and is, and is to come!" This is the reality behind reality. Whatever chaos reigns on earth, there is a throne in heaven, and it is occupied. God is not absent. He is not asleep. He reigns.

In the right hand of the One on the throne was a scroll sealed with seven seals — containing God's plan for the final redemption of all creation. A mighty angel proclaimed with a loud voice: "Who is worthy to break the seals and open the scroll?" And no one in heaven or on earth or under the earth could open it. John wept bitterly. Think about why: if no one can open the scroll, then evil wins. History remains unresolved. The martyrs die in vain. Injustice stands forever. The tears of every orphan, every victim, every oppressed soul remain unanswered. Without someone worthy to open the scroll, there is no justice, no resolution, no hope.

Seven trumpets sounded, each bringing intensifying warnings to a rebellious world. Hail and fire scorched the earth. A third of the sea turned to blood. Fresh water became bitter. Darkness swallowed portions of sky. These judgments echoed the plagues of Egypt — God using the same methods He used to liberate His people from Pharaoh, now deployed on a cosmic scale. But notice: they affected a third, not all. These were warnings, not final destruction. God's justice is not reckless rage — it is measured, purposeful, designed to awaken repentance. Even in judgment, mercy holds the door open. God would rather warn than destroy, rather discipline than condemn.

Every act of persecution, every empire that has tried to stamp out faith, every system that oppresses the vulnerable — behind it all is this ancient dragon, this serpent from Eden, who wages war against those who bear God's image.

The Moral of the Story / ናይ ዛንታ ትምህርቲ

The story of the world is not chaos — it has an Author and an ending. Evil is real, but it is temporary; God's justice will ultimately prevail. Suffering in this life is not the final chapter. Heaven is not escape from earth but earth made new — creation restored, relationships healed, purpose fulfilled. We live between "already" and "not yet" — the Kingdom has begun in the resurrection of Jesus, but it is not yet complete. This means we work and wait, we serve and hope, we fight against evil and rest in God's sovereignty simultaneously.

Hope is not wishful thinking; it is confidence in God's character and promises. The God who has been faithful throughout all of history — who kept His promise to Abraham, who delivered Israel from Egypt, who sent His Son to die and rise again — this same God has promised to make all things new. And He will. The Lamb who was slain holds the scroll of history. Every tear is counted. Every injustice will be answered. Every person who has suffered for faithfulness will be vindicated. And every power that has oppressed the vulnerable will be overthrown.

Until that day, we live as people of hope — not because our circumstances are easy, but because we know the Author of the story. We are not waiting for escape; we are waiting for renewal. And while we wait, we participate in that renewal by living as citizens of the Kingdom that is coming.
