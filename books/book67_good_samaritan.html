A lawyer stands up to test Jesus. This is not an attorney as we know today — he is an expert in the Torah, the Law of Moses. He is educated, respected, and confident in his own righteousness. He approaches Jesus not with genuine curiosity but with a trap disguised as a question: "Teacher, what must I do to inherit eternal life?" Notice the arrogance embedded in the question — "what must I do?" He assumes eternal life is something he can earn through his own effort, his own performance, his own religious achievement. He does not come hungry for truth; he comes to display his knowledge and expose what he assumes are flaws in Jesus's teaching. This is a public challenge, a theological debate in front of witnesses. But Jesus, as always, sees through the performance to the heart beneath. The lawyer wants to justify himself. Jesus wants to transform him. This tension between self-justification and genuine transformation is the engine that drives the entire parable that follows.

Jesus does not answer directly. Instead, with the wisdom that confounds the wise, He turns the question back: "What is written in the Law? How do you read it?" This is brilliant on multiple levels. First, Jesus acknowledges the man's expertise — you're the legal scholar, you tell me. Second, He refuses to be trapped into a theological debate on the lawyer's terms. Third, He reveals something profound: the answer to the greatest question of human existence — how to have eternal life — is not hidden or complicated. It is written plainly in the very Scripture this man studies every day. The problem is never that God's commands are unclear. The problem is that we look for loopholes rather than obedience. Jesus is essentially saying, "You already know the answer. The question is whether you're willing to live it." This pattern appears throughout Jesus's ministry — He rarely gives people new information. Instead, He confronts them with truth they already possess but refuse to apply.

The lawyer answers correctly — perfectly, in fact. He quotes the Shema from Deuteronomy 6:5 and combines it with Leviticus 19:18: "Love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, with all your strength, and with all your mind; and love your neighbor as yourself." Jesus affirms him: "You have answered correctly. Do this and you will live." The answer is elegant in its simplicity: love God completely, love people fully. Every commandment, every law, every prophetic word ultimately comes down to these two movements of the heart. But notice — Jesus says "DO this." Not "know this," not "recite this," not "believe this intellectually." DO it. Live it. The gap between knowing and doing is where most of us live our entire spiritual lives. This lawyer can quote the perfect answer, but as the next question reveals, he has spent his career finding ways to limit love rather than expand it. Head knowledge without heart obedience is worthless in the kingdom of God.

"But wanting to justify himself, he asked Jesus, 'And who is my neighbor?'" Luke tells us the motivation explicitly — he wants to justify himself. He wants to draw a circle around the command to make it manageable, controllable, limited. If "neighbor" only means people like me — my ethnicity, my religion, my social class — then I can check that box and feel righteous. This is perhaps the most human question in all of Scripture. We all want to know the minimum requirement. We all want boundaries around our compassion. How far does love have to extend? Who can I legitimately exclude? Who deserves my help and who doesn't? The lawyer is not asking an innocent question — he is looking for permission to withhold love from certain people. He wants Jesus to say "your neighbor is fellow Jews" so he can feel justified in his prejudice against everyone else. Instead, Jesus tells a story that will shatter every boundary the lawyer has ever built.

Jesus begins the parable: "A man was going down from Jerusalem to Jericho." With these simple words, Jesus paints a scene every listener would immediately recognize. The road from Jerusalem to Jericho was one of the most dangerous stretches in all of Judea — a steep descent of over 3,000 feet in just 17 miles, winding through barren wilderness and rocky ravines. It was nicknamed "the Way of Blood" because of the frequent robberies and murders that occurred there. Jesus does not identify the traveler's ethnicity, his religion, or his social status. He is simply "a man" — a human being, made in God's image. This is deliberate. Jesus strips away every label that might make the listener decide whether this person deserves compassion. He is not a Jew, a Gentile, a rich man, or a poor man in the story. He is a person. He is vulnerable. He is about to suffer. And that is all that should matter when we encounter someone in need.

The Jericho road was not just dangerous — it was notorious. The terrain was perfect for ambush — limestone caves provided hiding places, sharp turns blocked line of sight, and the desolate wilderness meant no witnesses and no help nearby. Violence happens. Suffering is real and often random. The question Jesus is really asking through this parable is not "why do bad things happen?" but "what do you do when you encounter someone who has been broken by this world?" The road to Jericho exists in every city, every school, every community. Someone near you right now is lying wounded on their own Jericho road.

"He fell among robbers, who stripped him of his clothing, wounded him, and departed, leaving him half dead." The violence is stark and total. They stripped him — taking not just his valuables but his very dignity. In the ancient world, clothing indicated identity, social status, and ethnicity. Without clothes, this man is unidentifiable. No one can tell if he is Jew or Gentile, rich or poor, righteous or sinner. They wounded him — the Greek word suggests repeated blows, a sustained beating. He cannot identify himself to potential rescuers. He can only lie there and wait — either for mercy or for death. This is the condition of every human being before God's grace reaches them. We are all, spiritually speaking, lying half dead on the Jericho road until someone crosses the distance to reach us.

To understand why "a Samaritan" was so shocking, you need the history. After Solomon's kingdom split around 930 BC, the northern kingdom of Israel eventually mixed with Assyrian settlers after their conquest in 722 BC. The resulting population — Samaritans — were viewed by Jews as racial and religious traitors. They had intermarried with pagans. They worshipped God on Mount Gerizim instead of Jerusalem. They accepted only the first five books of Moses. Jews and Samaritans had hated each other for over 700 years by Jesus's time. Jews would travel days out of their way to avoid crossing Samaritan territory. They considered Samaritan food unclean and Samaritan testimony invalid in court. A common Jewish prayer included thanking God for not making them a Samaritan. This hatred was not casual prejudice — it was deep, generational, religious, cultural, and personal. When Jesus made a Samaritan the hero, He was taking the most hated "other" in His audience's world and saying: "This person you despise? He understands God's heart better than your priests."

"And when he saw him, he was moved with compassion." The Greek word used here is "esplanchnisthe" — literally, his intestines churned. This is not mild sympathy or distant pity. This is visceral, gut-wrenching compassion that physically affects the body. It is the same word used to describe Jesus's compassion when He saw crowds like sheep without a shepherd, when He encountered the widow of Nain burying her only son, when He met the leper begging for healing. This is divine compassion flowing through a human heart. The Samaritan does not calculate whether helping is convenient. He does not check the man's ethnicity first. He does not weigh the risks or consider his schedule. He sees suffering and his insides twist with empathy. True compassion is not a thought — it is a physical response. It moves you before you have time to make excuses. The priest and Levite also "saw" — but seeing without compassion is just observation. The Samaritan saw AND felt. And feeling led to action.

The Moral of the Story

English: Your neighbor is anyone in need, regardless of their identity. Love is not a feeling to be reserved for those like you — it is an action to be extended to everyone, especially those your world tells you to despise. The question is never "who deserves my compassion?" but "am I willing to show it?" Go and do likewise.

Discussion Questions

1. Who are the "Samaritans" in your community — the people or groups that are looked down upon, avoided, or considered "other"? Be honest about the prejudices you carry. Where did those attitudes come from, and what would it look like to challenge them?

1. ኣብ ማሕበርካ "ሳማርያውያን" መን እዮም — ዝንዓቑ ዝርሕቑ ወይ "ካልእ" ዝቝጸሩ ሰባት ወይ ጉጅለታት? ብዛዕባ ትሰክሞ ኣድልዎ ቅኑዕ ኩን። ኣብይ ካብ ዝመጹ እዮም ንኣቶም ክትብድሆም ብኸመይ ይመስል?

2. The priest and Levite had religious reasons for passing by. What "religious excuses" do Christians today use to avoid showing compassion? How can devotion to God become an excuse for ignoring people in need?

2. ካህንን ሌዋውን ንምሕላፍ ሃይማኖታዊ ምኽንያት ነበሮም። ሎሚ ክርስትያናት ርሕራሔ ንከየርእዩ ዝጥቀሙሎም "ሃይማኖታዊ ምኽንያታት" እንታይ እዮም? ንኣምላኽ ምውፋይ ሓገዝ ዘድልዮም ሰባት ንምዝንጋዕ ምኽንያት ብኸመይ ክኸውን ይኽእል?

3. There is a difference between convenient compassion (helping when it's easy) and costly compassion (helping when it requires sacrifice). Give examples of each from your own life. Which type does Jesus call us to in this parable?

3. ኣብ መንጎ ምቹው ርሕራሔ (ቀሊል ምስ ዝኸውን ምሕጋዝ) ን ክቡር ርሕራሔ (መስዋእቲ ምስ ዝሓትት ምሕጋዝ) ን ፍልልይ ኣሎ። ካብ ናይ ገዛእ ርእስኻ ህይወት ንነፍሲ ወከፍ ኣብነት ሃብ። የሱስ ኣብዚ ምሳሌ ናብ ኣየናይ ዓይነት ይጽውዓና?

4. Jesus reframes the question from "who is my neighbor?" to "who was a neighbor?" How does this change our understanding of love and compassion? What does it mean that love is an identity rather than a transaction?

4. የሱስ ነታ ሕቶ ካብ "ብጻየይ መን እዩ?" ናብ "ብጻይ ዝኾነ መን ነበረ?" ይቕይራ። እዚ ብዛዕባ ፍቕርን ርሕራሔን ንዘለና ምርዳእ ብኸመይ ይቕይር? ፍቕሪ ካብ ልውውጥ ንላዕሊ መንነት ምዃኑ እንታይ ማለት እዩ?

5. "Go and do likewise" is a command, not a suggestion. What specific, practical steps can you take this week to cross boundaries of prejudice and show mercy to someone your world tells you to avoid? What will it cost you?

5. "ኪድ ከምኡ ግበር" ትእዛዝ እዩ ርእይቶ ኣይኮነን። ነዚ ሰሙን ናይ ኣድልዎ ደረታት ንምስጋር ዓለምካ ክትርሕቖ ዝነግረካ ሰብ ርሕራሔ ንምርኣይ ዝተፈልየ ግብራዊ ስጉምትታት እንታይ ክትወስድ ትኽእል? እንታይ ክኽፈለካ እዩ?

A Prayer for Boundless Compassion

Lord Jesus, You are the true Good Samaritan. You crossed the infinite distance between heaven and earth to reach us when we lay wounded and helpless on the road of sin. You did not pass by on the other side. You came to us, touched our wounds, bore our pain, and paid a price we could never repay. Thank You for Your mercy that knows no limits.

Forgive us for the times we have been the priest and the Levite — seeing need and crossing to the other side. Forgive us for our religious excuses, our convenient blindness, our selective compassion. Forgive us for drawing circles around our love and deciding who deserves it and who does not.

Give us eyes to see the wounded on our road. Open our hearts to feel the compassion that moves us to action, not just sympathy that leaves us comfortable. Give us the courage to cross every boundary — of prejudice, of tribalism, of comfort, of reputation — to reach those who are suffering.

Break down the walls of hatred we have built. Shatter our prejudices with Your love. Show us the image of God in every person — especially those we have been taught to despise. Help us remember that we were once the stranger lying half dead on the road, and someone crossed the distance to save us.

Make us neighbors to everyone we meet. Not because they deserve it, but because You command it. Not because it is easy, but because it is right. Not because we are good, but because You are good through us. May our compassion have no limits, our mercy no boundaries, and our love no conditions. In the name of Jesus, who showed us what true mercy looks like. Amen.
